Requirements for Students’ Skills

Analytic Geometry in Secondary‐School Curricula
Analytic geometry as part of the high‐school curriculum requires students to work with concepts of vector spaces, coordinate systems, and Euclidean metrics. Within national educational programs, it is expected that a student understands the notions of point, line, and plane, and is able to assess mutual positions of linear objects (and subsequently their images under free parallel projection). The curricula rely on a structure that fosters critical and logical thinking as well as the development of spatial imagination.

Innovative Methods in Teaching Stereometry and the Teacher’s Role
In this subsection, the author emphasizes that classic flat (2D) representations are insufficient to fully grasp spatial relationships. Spatial geometry can be enriched by adding innovative tools such as a 3D pen, paper ruler, or projection corner. The goal is for students to navigate relationships like line–plane, plane–plane, or spatial section through tactile and visual aids. A 3D pen allows the creation of a real model of a solid and its cross‐section, thereby developing students’ synthetic thinking and deepening their spatial imagination. The author claims that this “hands‐on” approach is especially useful for students with special educational needs and for those who learn better through practical rather than abstract means.

Introduction to Spatial Geometry in Secondary Schools
In the first chapter, the importance of developing spatial imagination in adolescent students is highlighted, since the cognitive development phase (ages 10–16) enables them to approach abstract stereometry concepts. Redechová presents an overview of the national curriculum (ŠVP) requirements on the topic of “Representing Three‐Dimensional Space,” which includes free parallel projection and constructing cross‐sections of solids. Students are expected to learn how to sketch cubes, cuboids, and pyramids and to assess mutual positions of lines and planes in space by examining their parallel (or skew) images on a plane.

Innovative Tools – 3D Pen
Redechová proposes integrating a 3D pen into teaching so that students can physically touch 3D models of solids and create their cross‐sections. Instead of purely theoretical reasoning about plane and solid, a student constructs a real pyramid, cube, or cuboid and then cuts it using a template made of paper or plastic. Thanks to this, they understand how cross‐sections form and perceive spatial cross‐sections “by hand” rather than only visually.

Research Parameters and Spatial Geometry in Elementary School
Trubačová begins by noting that in elementary schools (ages 10–12), one must still emphasize developing spatial imagination as a key literacy for modern individuals. The author explains that a 3D pen can bring abstract stereometry concepts closer to students through a simple gesture, as they can draw directly in space. In the chapter “Stereometry in Elementary Curricula,” it is detailed how teaching materials focus on basic relations—lines of intersection of planes, positions of lines and planes—helping students understand concepts like “parallel planes” or “intersection of a plane determined by three points.” For innovative education, the author suggests combining the 3D pen gesture (drawing pyramids, cubes, pendulum‐shaped chains) with traditional geometry exercises and templates for cutting a cube.
